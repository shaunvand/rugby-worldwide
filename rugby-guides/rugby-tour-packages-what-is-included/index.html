Rugby tour packages: what a tour includes and how to plan the budget

From flights and meals to fixtures and community service, here is what a rugby tour includes and how to plan the budget using published package prices.

What rugby tour packages usually include

Most coaches start with one question: what exactly is the money paying for? Good rugby tour packages answer that before anyone books a flight. At Rugby Management Worldwide, a tour is planned from start to finish, so the squad, the coaches and the parents know what is arranged and what is left for them to sort out.

A full tour plan includes the travel, the rugby and the time off the field. In practice that means:

  • flights to and from the destination
  • accommodation and meals
  • transfers between the airport, the hotel, training and match venues
  • match fixtures against schools and academies from other countries
  • training sessions
  • excursions and live pro matches
  • community service

Before you compare any tour, write down your own list of must-haves. A squad that wants hard, even fixtures needs something different from a squad on its first trip away. Knowing your priorities makes every conversation about price far easier.

Flights, accommodation, meals and transfers

Travel is usually the largest single part of any tour, and it is where good planning saves the most stress. Here is what to look for and ask about.

For flights, group bookings work best when names, passport details and dates of birth are collected early. Check that every passport is valid well beyond the return date and that minors have any consent letters or documents their destination asks for. Ask how much luggage is allowed, because kit bags, boots and team equipment add up quickly.

For accommodation, ask how rooms are shared, where coaches sleep in relation to players, and whether there is space for team meetings and video sessions. A quiet, secure base matters more than a fancy one.

Meals are easy to take for granted until a squad is playing two or three games in a weekend. Let the organiser know about allergies, religious requirements and players who need extra food. Good tours plan meals around fixtures and training, not the other way round.

Transfers are the hidden time-saver. When a bus is waiting at the airport and between venues, coaches can focus on the team instead of timetables and taxi apps.

Fixtures, training and the rugby itself

The rugby is the reason for the trip, so look closely at how fixtures are chosen. The aim is matches against schools and academies from other countries that test your players without turning every game into a mismatch. Share honest information about your squad: age group, playing level, recent results and how many players you are bringing.

A few practical points help coaches get the most from the rugby:

  • plan rotation so every player gets meaningful minutes
  • build rest into the schedule, especially after travel days
  • use training sessions to adjust to different conditions, pitches and styles of play
  • bring a basic medical kit and agree who handles injuries and insurance claims
  • keep a short review after each match so lessons stick

Festival tours work slightly differently. With an event such as the Dubai 7s or the Global Youth 7s, the fixtures come from the tournament draw, so preparation is about sevens fitness, a tight squad and players who can play several positions.

Excursions, live matches and community service

A rugby tour is also a chance for young players to see how others live and play. Excursions give the squad a break from the intensity of matches and help players and coaches bond away from the field. Watching live pro matches is often a highlight: players see the game at a higher level and pick up ideas they bring home.

Community service is part of the tour plan too. Time spent with local communities, such as coaching younger children, can be the part of the trip players remember longest. It also teaches the kind of respect and gratitude that coaches want to see on and off the field.

When reviewing a tour, check the balance. Too many excursions and the rugby suffers; too few and the squad misses the cultural side that makes touring worthwhile.

Reading the published package prices

The clearest way to plan is to start with the published prices on our tour packages page. For this year there are two festival packages, priced per player:

  • Dubai 7s: R 69 950 per SA player and R 45 000 per non-SA player, for a squad of 12 players (u19) and 2 coaches.
  • Global Youth 7s: R 112 500 per SA player and R 63 150 per non-SA player, for a squad of 14 players (u18) and 2 coaches.

Schools and academies can enter their own team. Individual players without a squad can also apply, because an invitational team is being entered as well.

For other destinations or a custom school tour, there is no fixed published price on that page, so send your tour idea and ask for a plan. Do not rely on figures from other sources or old brochures; check the current page and ask directly if anything is unclear.

How to plan the budget as a school or club

Once you have a package price, build a simple budget around it so families are not surprised later. Things to consider:

  • what is in the package price and what is not, confirmed in writing
  • spending money for players, with a sensible daily guideline agreed with parents
  • travel insurance and medical insurance
  • passport and visa fees where they apply
  • tour kit, such as matching travel wear and training tops
  • a small contingency for the unexpected

Give families a clear payment timeline and stick to it. Many schools hold fundraising events, approach local businesses for sponsorship or spread payments across the year. Start early: the longer the runway, the easier it is for every player to make the trip.

Finally, keep communication simple. One parent information evening, one shared document with dates and amounts, and one point of contact makes the whole process calmer.
