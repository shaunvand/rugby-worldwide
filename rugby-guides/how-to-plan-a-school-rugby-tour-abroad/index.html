How to plan a school rugby tour abroad: a coach checklist

From the first enquiry to the airport, here is a step-by-step checklist for coaches planning a school rugby tour abroad: dates, squad, fixtures, budget and parents.

By Rugby Management Worldwide · Updated 23 November 2026 · 5 min read

In this guide

  1. Start early and fix your dates
  2. Settle the party size and who travels
  3. Plan fixtures that suit your squad
  4. Building your school rugby tour budget
  5. Run a clear parents meeting
  6. Final checklist before departure

Start early and fix your dates

Ask any coach who has run a school rugby tour abroad and you will hear the same advice: start earlier than feels necessary. A good tour needs time for fixtures to be agreed, families to save and paperwork to be sorted. Twelve to eighteen months ahead is a sensible window for a long-haul trip, and even a shorter regional tour benefits from a full school year of preparation.

Begin with the school calendar. Block out exam periods, your own league and cup commitments, and the dates other sports need key players. Then look at the rugby calendar in the country you want to visit. Touring during their season means schools there are match fit and keen to play. Touring in their off-season can make good fixtures hard to find.

Think about the length of the trip as well. Somewhere between a week and a fortnight suits most school groups: long enough for several matches and some culture, short enough that players are not drained and parents are not stretched. Once you have two or three possible windows and a rough idea of destination, you are ready to make a first enquiry.

Settle the party size and who travels

Party size drives almost every other decision, from flights and accommodation to the number of fixtures you can sensibly play. Count everyone: players, coaches, managers and any parents or staff travelling with the group.

  • Players: take enough for injuries and rotation. For fifteens, a squad of roughly two full teams lets everyone play meaningful minutes across several matches. For a sevens festival, a squad of about twelve is typical.
  • Coaches: a head coach plus at least one assistant, so training can split into forwards and backs and one adult is always free to deal with problems.
  • Team manager: someone who handles logistics, money and communication with home, so the coaches can coach.
  • Medical support: a first-aid trained adult at minimum, ideally a physio or sports medic.
  • Supervision: follow your own school policy on adult-to-pupil ratios and safeguarding for trips abroad.

Decide early how players are selected and say so clearly. Is the tour open to everyone in the age group, or picked on merit? Is attendance at training before departure required? Clear criteria, shared in writing, avoid awkward conversations later.

Plan fixtures that suit your squad

The rugby is the heart of the tour, so be honest about your level. Three heavy defeats can sour a trip, and three easy wins teach nothing. Aim for opposition that will test your players without swamping them.

When you make your first enquiry, give the age grades, a realistic picture of your results this season and the style of rugby you play. That helps whoever arranges fixtures to match you with schools and academies of a similar standard. Ask for a mix: perhaps one fixture you expect to win, one close contest and one stretch game against stronger opposition.

Build in rest. Contact matches back to back, on hard grounds and after long travel days, lead to injuries. A good rhythm is a match, then a training or excursion day, then another match. Use training sessions on tour to sharpen set pieces and pick up ideas from local coaches.

Building your school rugby tour budget

Parents will ask one question before any other: how much? Get a clear, itemised quote before you announce dates. A full tour package usually includes flights, accommodation and meals, transfers, fixtures, training, excursions and match tickets. Check exactly what is in and what is not.

Items that are often left out of early sums include:

  • Travel insurance that includes rugby injuries and medical care
  • Passports, visas and any vaccinations
  • Tour kit: playing shirts, training wear, travel polos and kitbags
  • Spending money and meals on travel days
  • Gifts for host schools, such as pennants or shirts
  • A contingency fund for the unexpected

Set a payment plan with clear deadlines, usually a deposit followed by instalments. Spreading payments makes the trip possible for more families. Fundraising helps too: sponsored events, a supporters dinner, local business sponsorship of the tour kit, or a raffle. Agree early how money raised is shared, whether equally across the squad or credited to the players who raised it. Keep transparent records and report to parents regularly.

Run a clear parents meeting

Hold a parents meeting once dates, destination and an estimated cost are known, and before you ask for deposits. It sets the tone for the whole tour, so prepare properly.

Go through these points:

  1. The purpose of the tour: rugby, culture, community service and growing as people.
  2. Dates, destination and a draft itinerary.
  3. Total cost, what it includes and the payment schedule.
  4. Selection criteria and squad size.
  5. Supervision, safeguarding and the code of conduct on tour.
  6. Medical forms, allergies, dietary needs and insurance.
  7. How parents will hear news while the group is away.

Leave plenty of time for questions, and send a written summary afterwards so families who missed the meeting have the same information. Set up a single channel for tour updates, such as a parents group on WhatsApp, and name one person who answers questions. Players should hear the code of conduct directly from the coach too, so everyone knows what is expected before a bag is packed.

Final checklist before departure

In the last few weeks, work through a short list:

  • All passports valid and visas approved where needed
  • Insurance confirmed, with policy details carried by staff
  • Medical consent forms, allergy lists and emergency contacts for every player
  • Rooming lists, daily schedule and kit list sent to families
  • Kit numbered and packed, with first-aid supplies, strapping and spare boots
  • Tour rules agreed with players and signed
  • Contact details for hosts, transport and the tour organiser
