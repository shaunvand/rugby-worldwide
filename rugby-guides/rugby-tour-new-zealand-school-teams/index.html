Rugby tour New Zealand: what school teams should know

New Zealand is one of the great places to take a school rugby team. Here is why it works, how a fixture week fits together and how to prepare players and parents.

Why school teams look to New Zealand

A rugby tour New Zealand trip puts your school team in one of the great rugby nations in the world. Rugby there is built on skills, speed of thought and a deep school and club culture. For a touring side, that means opposition who handle the ball confidently, support every break and punish loose kicking. Playing against that style for a week teaches more about decision-making than a whole term of drills at home.

There is also the off-field side. Rugby sits at the centre of community life in many New Zealand towns, so touring teams are often welcomed warmly by host schools, local clubs and families. Young players see how the game is coached, refereed and supported in another country, and they come home with fresh ideas and a clearer sense of what good rugby looks like.

For coaches, a tour is a chance to build a squad culture that lasts the whole season. Travelling, eating, training and recovering together shows quickly who leads, who needs support and how the group handles pressure.

Planning a rugby tour New Zealand trip: timing, squad and budget

Start early. Long-haul flights, school calendars and fixture availability all need lining up, so give yourself plenty of lead time before your planned travel window.

  • Pick your timing carefully. Check when New Zealand schools are in term and in season, and match that against your own school holidays. Touring when host schools are playing gives you the strongest fixtures.
  • Decide your squad size. Take enough players to cover injuries and rotate fairly across matches, plus enough adults for proper supervision.
  • Agree the purpose. Is the tour about winning, development, or giving a wider group of players a first taste of international rugby? The answer shapes the fixtures you ask for.
  • Talk money openly with parents. Share a clear payment plan and what is included, and start any fundraising early.

Long flights mean jet lag is real. Build a light first day into the plan, with a gentle run, a stretch and early nights, before asking players to compete.

What a fixture week looks like

A well-planned tour week balances matches, training, recovery and experiences. A typical shape might be:

  1. Arrival and settling in. Transfers to accommodation, a light session to shake off the flight, and a team meeting on standards and the week ahead.
  2. Training day. A focused session to sharpen set pieces and calls, ideally with some time on the pitch you will play on.
  3. First fixture. A match against a host school or academy, followed by time with the opposition afterwards. These moments often become the best memories of the trip.
  4. Recovery and excursion. Pool or beach recovery in the morning, then a cultural or sightseeing outing.
  5. Community service. A morning giving something back, such as running a coaching session for younger players.
  6. Second fixture and a live professional match. Play in the afternoon and, where the calendar allows, watch top-level rugby in the evening.
  7. Travel day. Pack, review the week together as a squad and head home.

Two or three matches in a week is usually enough for school players. Packing in extra games tends to raise injury risk and leaves little energy for the experiences that make touring worthwhile.

Visiting the national test stadium in Auckland

Auckland is where many tours begin and end, and a visit to the famous test stadium in the city is a highlight for most players. This is a ground steeped in international rugby history, and standing in the stands or walking near the pitch helps young players connect their own season to the biggest stage in the game.

Our own photos from on tour in Auckland show what that moment means: squads lined up for team pictures, players taking in the size of the place and coaches using it as a backdrop for a talk about where hard work can lead.

A few tips for the visit:

  • Book stadium tours in advance, as access depends on the event calendar.
  • Wear team tracksuits for photos so the squad looks like a squad.
  • Brief players beforehand on the history of the ground, so the visit is not just a photo stop.
  • If a professional match is on during your week, ask whether tickets can be part of the plan.

Preparing players and parents

Good preparation at home makes the tour itself run smoothly.

  • Passports and visas. Check passport expiry dates early and confirm the entry requirements for your nationality well before booking.
  • Medical and insurance. Collect medical forms, allergy details and consent forms, and make sure travel insurance covers contact sport.
  • Kit list. Layers matter, as the weather can change quickly in a single day. Pack boots with both moulded and screw-in studs, a warm top, a rain jacket and spare playing socks.
  • Conditioning. Players should arrive fit. A tour is not the time to get fit.
  • Behaviour and phones. Agree squad rules on curfews, phones and social media before departure, and share them with parents.
  • Communication. Set up a parent group so families get regular updates and photos during the trip.

Parents worry less when they know the itinerary, who is supervising and how to reach the team. A short pre-tour meeting covering these points is time well spent.

Quick answers

How long should a school rugby tour to New Zealand be?

Long enough to recover from long-haul flights and play two or three matches with proper rest between them. Leave room for training, an excursion, community service and a live professional match if the calendar allows.

Can individual players join a tour?

Yes. Rugby Management Worldwide enters an invitational team, so players looking for a team to join can still tour and compete.

What does a tour include?

Tours are planned from start to finish and include flights, accommodation and meals, transfers, match fixtures, training sessions, excursions, live pro matches and community service.

When should we start planning?

As early as you can. Flights, school calendars, fixtures and parent fundraising all take time, so share your team type, destination, rough dates and party size with us well ahead of travel.
