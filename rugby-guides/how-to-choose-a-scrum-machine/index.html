How to choose a scrum machine for your school or club

Who uses a scrum machine, and why

A scrum machine is one of the biggest single purchases a school or club rugby programme will make, so it pays to choose carefully. The right machine gives your forwards a safe, repeatable way to practise body position, binding and leg drive without the fatigue and risk of endless live scrums. The wrong one sits in a shed because it is too heavy to move, too big for your juniors or too basic for your senior pack.

Most machines are bought for forwards, but a wider group uses them than you might expect:

  • Front rows working on height, binding and a stable base before they scrum live.
  • Locks and loose forwards practising their push, foot position and timing with the front row.
  • Whole squads using a sled for contact, leg drive and conditioning at the end of a session.
  • Young players who are new to scrummaging and need confidence before they face an opposing pack.

For parents, a good machine in the hands of a careful coach is reassuring. It means scrum technique is taught step by step instead of learned the hard way on match day.

Sleds or multi-person machines

Scrum equipment falls broadly into two groups. Sleds are smaller units built for one player or a small group, while multi-person machines are built for a front row or a full pack to engage at once.

Sleds

A sled is the natural starting point for many schools. It suits individual technique, contact work and leg drive, and it is usually easier to move and store. Match the machine to your players

Start with the players you actually coach, not the pack you hope to have in a few seasons.

  • Age and size. Junior and younger school teams need a machine they can engage correctly. A machine built for heavy senior packs can teach poor habits if small players strain to shift it.
  • Number of teams. A school running several age groups may get better value from a sled plus a junior machine than from one large machine that only the top team uses.
  • Coaching time. If forwards get one short session a week, a simple sled used well beats a complex machine used rarely.
  • Ambition. Clubs and schools with dedicated forwards coaches and regular scrum sessions will get full use from a larger machine.

Many programmes build up in stages: a sled first, then a multi-person machine once numbers and coaching support grow. That spreads the cost and lets you learn what your players really need.

Space, transport and storage

A machine is only useful if it reaches the field easily and comes back in good condition.

  • Training space. Mark out where the machine will be used. Allow room in front for the pack to set up and behind for the machine to travel when driven. Keep it away from other groups, posts and fences.
  • Surface. Check whether your training surface suits a sled or wheeled machine. Natural grass, artificial turf and sandy fields all behave differently, especially when wet.
  • Moving it. Ask how many people it takes to shift the machine, and whether you can do it safely without a vehicle. A light sled can be dragged by a few players; larger machines need planning.
  • Transport. If you want to take equipment to camps or away venues, find out whether it fits in a bakkie or trailer before you buy.
  • Storage. A lockable shed or garage near the field is ideal. Sun and winter rain wear down pads and frames, so keep the machine dry and out of direct sun between sessions.

Give one coach or staff member responsibility for the machine. A simple monthly check of pads, bolts and frame keeps it safe and working for longer.

Safety and coaching use

A machine does not coach players. The person running the session does. Good practice looks the same at every level:

  1. Qualified supervision. Scrum work should always be led by a coach who knows correct technique. Players should never use the machine unsupervised.
  2. Warm up properly. Include neck, shoulder and hip mobility before any contact or pushing.
  3. Body position first. Teach a flat back, head up, hips below shoulders and feet in a stable base before adding force.
  4. Build gradually. Progress from single players on a sled, to pairs, to a front row, then a full pack. Only move on when technique holds.
  5. Short, quality reps. Several short engagements with rest between them teach far better than long, tiring pushes where form breaks down.
  6. Check the equipment. Look at pads, frame and moving parts before every session, and take the machine out of use if something is damaged.

Players should wear a well-fitted mouthguard for contact work. The RHINO Dual Gel Mouthguard is R 139.99 in our store, and the RHINO Pro Head Guard (R 749.99) is available for players and parents who want headgear.

Use the machine to support live scrummaging, not replace it. Players still need carefully managed live practice against opponents of similar size and experience.

Quick answers

What is the difference between a scrum sled and a scrum machine?

A sled is a smaller unit built for one player or a small group, suited to technique, contact and leg drive. A multi-person scrum machine lets a front row or full pack engage together and practise as a unit.

Which scrum machine suits a junior school team?

Look for a machine young players can engage correctly without straining. The RHINO Rebel 3 Junior Scrum Machine is aimed at younger packs, and a sled works well for individual technique. Check the product details or ask us before ordering.

Can players use a scrum machine without a coach?

No. Scrum training should always be supervised by a coach who knows correct technique, with a proper warm-up and gradual progression from individual work to full pack engagements.
